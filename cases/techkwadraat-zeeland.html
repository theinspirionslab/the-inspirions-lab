Techkwadraat Zeeland

PMO+ for Techkwadraat Zeeland
From programme structure to regional capacity

A working exploration of how structure, collaboration and available resources can create more meaningful encounters between children and technology.

Why this case exists

Techkwadraat Zeeland already has a plan, programme lines and funded activities. The challenge is therefore not simply to create another programme.

The interesting question is how available capacity, partners, ideas and funding can actually become experiences for children — without adding another layer of work to schools and teachers.

This page is a working exploration of that question from a PMO+ perspective.

Reading the assignment

The formal assignment is clear: create overview, support planning and reporting, monitor progress, finances and risks, strengthen cooperation and help regional coordinators turn ideas into executable initiatives.

But underneath that operational assignment may be a different systems problem.

Schools already encounter many organisations that want to bring knowledge, technology, experiences and new perspectives to children. The scarce resource may therefore not be the supply of ideas or activities. It may be the capacity of schools to absorb them.

The design question

How might Techkwadraat make existing energy easier to access, connect and scale — so that schools do not have to navigate a fragmented landscape of providers, projects and interventions?

This does not necessarily mean creating one central supplier. It may mean creating one clear regional point of access to a diverse ecosystem of people, materials, interventions and opportunities.

Single source of access — not necessarily single source of supply.

A fragmented landscape

Different organisations may already approach the same underlying challenge from different directions:

  • External intervention — a specialist comes into the school and creates an experience, while giving the teacher temporary space.
  • Ready-to-use learning concepts — materials, structure and teaching methods enable teachers to continue independently.
  • Supported transfer — material, expertise and guidance are combined until the school can take over.
  • Direct encounters — children meet professionals, companies, technologies and real-world applications.

These do not necessarily need to compete. They may be different answers to the same constraint: limited school capacity.

From programme control to programme capacity

A strong PMO does not create bureaucracy. It creates clarity, focus and room for action.

Structure can therefore have a second function: creating capacity.

If planning, information, partners, available interventions and results become easier to connect, less energy is spent on coordination — and more becomes available for creating meaningful encounters with children.

Methodologies such as Agile or Prince2 are therefore tools, not goals. The question is always whether a structure helps people act, learn and improve.

The PMO+ role

The PMO+ function can act as a connecting layer between:

available funding → regional initiatives → partners → schools → experiences → learning → measurable impact

The objective is not to centralise everything. It is to make the regional ecosystem easier to navigate and easier to activate.

  • Creating a shared picture of progress, capacity and dependencies.
  • Supporting regional coordinators without adding unnecessary administrative burden.
  • Making knowledge, partners and successful interventions reusable.
  • Turning information into insights for better decisions.
  • Identifying risks, bottlenecks and opportunities early.
  • Helping promising ideas become small, executable experiments.
  • Creating feedback between what happens in practice and how the programme develops.

Working hypotheses

These are hypotheses, not conclusions. They should be tested against the reality of schools, coordinators and partners.

  • Structure should create capacity, not administration.
  • The scarce resource may be school capacity, not ideas or funding.
  • Existing initiatives may become more powerful when they are easier to discover, connect and deploy.
  • A regional programme does not need to own every intervention; it needs to make the right intervention easier to reach.
  • Small experiments can reveal what deserves to be scaled.
  • Every intervention should ultimately answer one question: what changes for the child?

From planning to learning

A regional programme does not need every answer before it starts. The plan provides direction; implementation provides information.

Work can therefore become progressively more concrete:

6 months → 3 months → 6 weeks → 3 weeks → 6 days → 3 days

The further the horizon moves towards action, the more specific the commitments, dependencies and available capacity become. Reserved blocks create focus; short feedback loops create learning.

The aim is not to predict everything. It is to make the next useful action visible — and create enough space for the next idea to emerge.

What ultimately matters

Programme structure is not the outcome.

Reports, roadmaps, meetings and project plans are only useful if they help create more and better opportunities for children to encounter technology, science, making and innovation.

The ultimate chain is simple:

encounter → curiosity → experience → participation → development → future talent

The administrative system exists to make that chain possible, not the other way around.

The Inspirions Lab perspective

This case illustrates a recurring question within The Inspirions Lab:

Why do we organise things the way we do — and what would happen if we designed the system around the scarce resource instead?

In this case, the scarce resource may be the time and attention available within schools.

Structure then has a different purpose. It is not there to control complexity. It is there to make meaningful action easier.

PMO+ Principles

Structure

Create overview, focus and capacity without unnecessary bureaucracy.

Connection

Make it easier for schools, partners and initiatives to find each other.

Learning

Use small experiments and feedback to discover what deserves to scale.
